# Molecular Interactions and Surface Chemistry {#mpkb:section:sec-0223}

At an interface, molecular behavior depends on interactions among molecules and between molecules
and the surrounding surface or material. These interactions influence adsorption, orientation,
mobility, recognition, reaction, and the organization of surface-bound structures. Their relative
importance depends on the molecular building blocks and the chemical and physical state of the
interface.

## Intermolecular and Surface Interactions {#mpkb:section:sec-0230}

Relevant interactions include electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, dispersion forces,
hydrophobic effects, coordination, and covalent attachment. A substrate can also affect molecular
organization through its surface chemistry, crystal structure, charge, defects, and roughness.
Consequently, an assembly pattern cannot always be predicted from molecule–molecule interactions
alone.

Solvent, concentration, temperature, pH, ionic strength, electrode potential, and illumination can
alter adsorption and association. At solid–liquid interfaces, both the solution and the substrate
participate in the process. These conditions should be treated as part of the system description,
not as incidental details.

## Recognition and Surface Organization {#mpkb:section:sec-0231}

Molecular recognition is selective association based on complementary chemical or structural
features. On surfaces, recognition can produce ordered monolayers, supramolecular networks, or
host–guest arrangements. Assembly may approach equilibrium or become kinetically trapped; observed
order alone does not establish which interactions produced it.

Surface chemistry is used in molecular programming to attach components, control their orientation
or spacing, and connect molecular devices to larger materials. The interface can also introduce
unwanted effects, including nonspecific adsorption, aggregation, altered activity, or restricted
mobility.

## Measurement and Experimental Design {#mpkb:section:sec-0232}

Scanning probe methods, including scanning tunneling microscopy and atomic force microscopy, can
resolve surface structures at small scales. Spectroscopy and other surface-sensitive methods can
provide complementary information about chemical composition and binding. Measurements should be
selected to test both the proposed structure and the interaction mechanism.

Useful controls include changing one interaction motif at a time, comparing different substrates,
and varying solution or environmental conditions. These controls help separate molecule–molecule
effects from molecule–surface effects and identify whether an observed pattern is robust or
condition-specific.
